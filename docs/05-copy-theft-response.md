# 05 · Fighting Copy-Theft Calmly, Without Lawyers

Someday someone will resell your planner under their own name, or hand a
buyer's-friend copy of your PDF to a Discord for free, and you will find it at
11 p.m. This chapter is the protocol to have preloaded. The whole approach:
**assume good faith first, escalate slowly, document everything, and never
throw more hours at a dispute than the product was worth.**

## Step 0 — Verify it's actually yours

Copy-theft responses fail when they're lazy. Before you message anyone,
collect:

- **Proof of priority.** Earlier, dated copies of your work: your git history,
  your build folder, an email to yourself, the public listing with its
  "opened shop" date. Start from the earliest thing you can date.
- **Evidence of copying, not coincidence.** Identical sample text, the same
  typos, same page order, your footer line. Two "morning routine" worksheets
  are a coincidence; an identical *layout and sentences* is not.
- **The realistic scale.** Are they selling 4 copies or 400? Did they take the
  *file* — which you can act on — or just the *idea*, which nobody can?

## Step 1 — the polite note (works more often than it should)

Most small-time theft is someone who found your work bundled somewhere,
decided it was fine, and never thought of it as theft. Open calm and assume
confusion:

> "Hi — I'm the author of [product name], posted in [month, year] here:
> [link to your listing]. It looks like [their link] is reselling it. Would
> you take the listing down, or point buyers at my licensed page instead so
> they get the current files? Happy to talk if this was an honest mix-up."

No lawyers, no threats, no capital letters. **Set a calendar reminder for
seven days** — most of these end here, quietly.

## Step 2 — the formal takedown ladder

If seven days pass with no reply:

1. **Platform report.** Etsy, Gumroad, and Ko-fi each have an infringement or
   misuse report form (search the platform name plus "report infringement"
   for the current link). State it plainly: "This listing resells my digital
   product [their URL]. Here is my original [URL], my dated files, and my
   license text."
2. **DMCA-style notice.** For sites without a good form — a personal website
   reselling your PDF, for example — you can send your own takedown notice.
   In round numbers it's: your name, a description of your work, the host's
   address (the hosting company's abuse email or DMCA agent), the infringing
   URLs, a statement of good-faith belief in your claim, and your signature.
   You do **not** need a lawyer, and you do **not** need a copyright
   registration to send one.
3. **Payment or host pressure, rarely.** If someone runs their own site
   selling stolen files, a short, factual letter to their payment processor
   or web host — with evidence attached — usually works faster than any
   copyright route, because those companies care about their own liability,
   not yours.

**What not to do:** never file a claim you aren't sure about (false takedowns
can boomerang), never escalate mid-conversation with insults, and never mob
their reviews or their buyers.

## Step 3 — budget your outrage

A loss-based triage table, in round numbers:

| What the theft costs you | What to spend |
| --- | --- |
| Maybe a few sales a month | The polite note and a calendar alert; nothing else |
| A sloppy clone with weak evidence | Let it go. Ideas aren't protected, and that's fine — you out-create them |
| Real money, straight file copy | Full ladder: note → platform report → notice |
| They mock you publicly | Stop talking privately; run the paper trail and post one warm factual line for your own audience |

And the standing rule: **never pay anyone for "legal services" in a $10
printables dispute.** The fees cost more than you could ever recover.

## Step 4 — make future theft boring

Each of these takes about an afternoon, once per product family, and quietly
shrinks any future incident:

- **License in the file.** Every download carries the plain-English license
  page ([Chapter 4](04-licenses-in-plain-english.html)), so even recipients
  of a pirated copy learn whom it belongs to — sometimes they buy.
- **Preview images at "screenshot" resolution.** Slides ~1200px wide; actual
  product pages crisp. A thief building a product from your previews builds a
  visibly poorer product.
- **A light watermark on previews only.** A subtle "sample — full version in
  the download" diagonal on preview images. Never watermark paid pages: that
  punishes exactly the people who paid.
- **Small dated footers.** Put "v2.2026 · Asdale Dynamics" in a footer line
  of each product page. Old copies eventually say something your current
  version doesn't, which makes sorting your copies from theirs trivial.
- **Version numbers that improve visibly.** Thieves copying v1 will be stuck
  selling v1 forever while your v2 rolls out. Free marketing for your listing.

## Keep the search habit small

Check for your own titles quarterly, in ten minutes, not weekly in an hour:
search your product name, the distinctive sentence from your license page,
and one distinctive filename. Finding theft is cheap; dwelling on it is
expensive. Write the note, send the report, close the tab, and go finish the
next launch checklist.
