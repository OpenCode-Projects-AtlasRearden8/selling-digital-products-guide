# 01 · What to Make Besides Books

A book is one possible container for your knowledge, and historically not the
most profitable one. This chapter is the menu: what sells well as a download,
what fits a one-person LLC, and what to skip no matter how good the idea sounds.

## Three honest questions before building anything

Run every idea through these, or through the
[scorecard worksheet](scorecard.html):

1. **Is the buyer a person who already exists near me?** Readers of your book,
   new nurses following your AI explainers, teachers who found your printables.
   "Someone somewhere wants it" is not a customer.
2. **Does it take an afternoon to make, not a month?** Digital products that
   require actual software development stop being products and start being
   jobs. Save apps for the open-source shelf.
3. **Will it sell for $5–$25 while requiring zero hand-holding?** Anything that
   needs configuration help, custom work, or tech support per sale belongs in a
   different (probably consulting-shaped) business.

## The Big Three formats

### Templates

Pre-filled documents that ask a buyer "here, start from this" instead of a
blank page. This is where your AI-author skills translate directly:

- **Prompt packs and prompt worksheets** — a tidy PDF or text file of prompts
  that actually work, organized by task ("summarize a research paper," "draft a
  listing description," "plan a nursing shift"). Beginner-friendly and aligned
  with everything Asdale Dynamics publishes. Price point: usually $5–$15.
- **Book-launch templates** — the checklist, email series, and A+ content
  sheets you already built for your own books, cleaned up for strangers.
- **Budget and bookkeeping spreadsheets** — a one-tab spreadsheet beats a
  seven-tab monster. "Simple" is a feature buyers pay for.

### Printables

Single-page or multi-pagePDFs bought mostly on Etsy to be *printed at home*.
The format rewards you for caring about margins and ink:

- Chore charts, habit trackers, and ADHD-friendly "one small thing" checklists.
- Paperwork organizers: "get the papers into one visible folder" is basically a
  printable promise — medical-prep sheets, shift-report organizers,
  annual-household binders.
- Study aids aimed at nursing students and new nurses (educational only, never
  clinical advice — say so on the listing).

Printables usually sell **$3–$8**. Small prices, almost no support burden, and
they bundle beautifully (see [Chapter 6](06-seasonal-bundles.html)).

### Planners

A printable's ambitious sibling: dated or undated multi-page systems, 20–60
pages, sold **$8–$25**. The trap is completeness — buyers drown in 90-page
planners with a 40-page "how to use me" section. A planner the size of a
pamphlet that covers *three pages a day, one-line entries* sells over and over
to exactly the people who bounce off big planners.

## What to skip (for now)

- **Anything that needs updating weekly.** Marketplace fee tables change; rate
  calculators expire; "current best AI tools" lists date like milk. Keep those
  as free lead magnets instead of paid products.
- **Personal-data-shaped templates** that ask buyers to type sensitive info
  into your files. A medical organizer that stores doses and diagnoses invites
  questions you want no part of. Collection is fine; storage is not.
- **Anything sold as a subscription until you have ~100 happy single-purchase
  customers.** Subscriptions turn a friendly download into a support contract.
- **Reselling other people's assets.** Even PLR bundles. Your license pages
  (see [Chapter 4](04-licenses-in-plain-english.html)) only work because the
  content is genuinely yours.

## Make one, then make the cousin

Your second product almost writes itself once the first exists: a companion
printable for your planner, a refill pack for your prompt workbook, the
teacher edition of the shift organizer. Cousin products let you bundle,
cross-list, and reuse preview templates — that compounding is the actual strategy.
